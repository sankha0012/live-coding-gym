This kind of drift is silent: the values are still valid numbers, so nothing raises an error, and the labels come a day late, so accuracy cannot be my early signal. I monitor the inputs and the outputs instead. For each important feature I compare a live window with a reference window from training, using the mean and the spread measured in units of the reference standard deviation, or a distribution distance such as PSI. I also watch the distribution of the predicted delivery times, because a shift in the inputs usually shows up there too. In the preparation-time case the mean of that feature would move beyond the limit within the first window and the alarm would name the feature. To avoid an alarm every hour I use a window large enough to be stable, a threshold that tolerates normal daily and weekly cycles, and I require the condition to persist for a few windows before paging someone. The trade-off is sensitivity against noise: a tight limit catches drift early but wakes people up for nothing. When the alarm fires, the next step is to check the upstream change, compare with labels once they arrive, and decide whether to retrain or roll back.
