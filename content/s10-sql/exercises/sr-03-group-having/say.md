FROM orders reads the eight rows. WHERE status = 'delivered' runs first and removes the two cancelled orders (ids 3 and 5), leaving six rows. GROUP BY restaurant_id builds one group per restaurant: 7 has two rows, 8 has one, 9 has three. HAVING then filters whole groups using aggregates: a group survives only if it has at least two orders and a revenue above 80. Restaurant 8 fails on count (and its revenue is only 30). SELECT computes the count and sum, and ORDER BY revenue DESC sorts the survivors.

I ran it and it returns two rows: restaurant 9 with n_orders 3 and revenue 140.0, then restaurant 7 with n_orders 2 and revenue 120.0.

The trap is the difference between WHERE and HAVING. WHERE sees individual rows before grouping, so the cancelled order of restaurant 7 never enters its count or sum; its 120.0 is 50 + 70, not 140. If the status test were removed entirely, restaurant 7 would show 3 orders and 140.0 (the cancelled 20 included) and restaurant 8 would show 2 orders and 75.0, which fails the revenue test but only by luck of the threshold. Moving the status test into HAVING is not valid on a bare column in strict engines such as PostgreSQL, because status is neither grouped nor aggregated (SQLite tolerates it and returns an arbitrary row's value). Likewise a bare row-level column like total in HAVING has no single value per group. Putting a condition in the wrong clause changes the numbers, not just the speed.
