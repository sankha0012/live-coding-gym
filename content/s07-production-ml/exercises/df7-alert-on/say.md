I alert in three layers, because each one catches a different failure. First, service health: error rate, latency percentiles such as p95 and p99, and the fallback rate. These say the service is up, but not that it is right, as this incident showed. Second, data health on the inputs: schema and unit checks, the share of missing values, and a distribution check on each key feature against a training baseline. A distance that suddenly is a thousand times larger would trip a simple range or drift alert within minutes, and that is the alert that would have caught this incident. Third, model health: the distribution of the predictions, and the real error once the labels arrive. Since labels come 40 to 90 minutes later, the real error confirms and cannot be the first alarm. I only alert on things someone can act on, with a threshold set from a baseline over normal days and a minimum sample size, and I page for severe problems and send the rest to a daily report so the alerts are not ignored. Each alert has an owner and a runbook, for example check the upstream change and switch to the fallback.
